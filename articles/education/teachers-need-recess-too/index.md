Think of a teacher standing in the doorway of your classroom. They’re not holding a clipboard. They’re not there because of a scheduled peer-teaching session. They’re there because a free period wasn’t swallowed by admin, and

you both are so comfortable with pop-ins that them choosing to spend a free period in your classroom feels completely normal.

In a healthy school, that is the norm. Unfortunately in a lot of schools, the teacher at the front tenses immediately. *Why are you in my class? Don’t you have something better to do?*

Maybe they wanted to sit in your class to see how you’re tackling the same maths concept they’ve spent a week on. Not because they want to sit at the back and judge how you run your room.

The difference between those two reactions is the whole difference between a collaborative school and an audited one.

#### **The best ideas were handed to me sneakily**

*When I think about the best ideas I’ve picked up as a teacher, almost none of them came from formal PD. They came from popping into a fellow teacher’s classroom and seeing what she was doing. They came from sitting in a coffee shop on a break, venting that I couldn’t get my kids to grasp a concept, and a teacher from another grade going, “Hey, have you tried this?”*

Nobody scheduled those moments. Nobody logged them. They happened because two people had nothing in their hands and time to be human beings with each other.

That’s where ideas come from: freedom. The 45 unscheduled minutes where your brain isn’t executing a plan or falling behind admin work.

## **Observation killed the cat**

Management tends to treat scheduled synergy sessions and peer-teaching opportunities as something added on top of everyday collaboration. They aren’t. They replace it. The message of the act is altered, simply by being observed.

If the only reason a fellow teacher ever sits in your classroom is to satisfy KPI’s, every visitor becomes an observer. The exact same act, a colleague sitting at the back watching your lesson, means support and care in one school and surveillance in another. Formalise it, and you don’t get more accountability. You convert organic collaboration into measured outcome, and every teacher in the building feels the switch.

The pressure doesn’t stay in the staffroom, either. It walks into the classroom with them.

*“When a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure.” — Marilyn Strathern*

### **Practice what you preach**

Here’s what gets me. Look at what we ask teachers to do with children.

Teachers are told to differentiate, to scaffold, to hold brain breaks, to treat every student as an individual. We build circle time and recess into the day because we know that children need unstructured time. Leave kids in a room with nothing scheduled and a mayhem worthy of the lighter parts of *Lord of the Flies* will ensue, not because they’re doing anything wrong, but because they’ll fill that time with something, and that something comes out of *them.* That’s where their creativity lives.

Teachers — despite some academic coordinators’ objections — are of the same make. There is no developmental stage where unstructured time suddenly becomes wasted like in *Momo and the Time Thieves.*

Yet the same schools that champion less homework for children send their teachers home with backpacks bursting with benign admin, much of which could be offloaded to AI. Every minute accounted for. Everyone visibly busy. As if every minute not spent doing is a minute wasted. That’s not a management philosophy. It’s a double standard, and our own pedagogy is the evidence against it.

#### **Recess isn’t unstructured. It’s freedom by design.**

The usual objection is that unstructured time gets wasted and some teachers will coast by. As someone who coasted through Finnish comprehensive in the early 2000s, I know this to be true, but also recess isn’t actually unstructured. It’s bounded: a set time, a set space, a duty teacher at the edge of the playground, and complete freedom inside that container. Nobody decides what the kids play. Somebody decided that they get to play.

As for the coasters: everyone sees them, the students, the faculty, fellow teachers and management. They don’t require synergy sessions to keep from drowning, and my argument isn’t meant as the perfect solution to every edge case.

#### **Teachers need unstructured opportunities to collaborate:**

* **Overlapping free periods,** so teachers from the same grade, or from the grades either side, are actually free at the same time.
* **A staffroom worth sitting in,** not a cupboard with a kettle.
* **An open-door policy through the whole institution,** so a pop-in is so ordinary it’s boring.
* **Leaders who pop in for the right reasons. “**I love these interactive notebooks you guys are building! Want to show me?” Every time a manager walks into a classroom curious instead of evaluating, the door gets a little easier to open for everyone else.

None of this costs much, and in my opinion school culture plays a huge role in teacher retention. A revolving door is an expensive problem.

### **What we lose when everything is formal**

Let me preface this with: formal observations will never go away. I am not arguing against them, I have even planned rubrics for a few of them. I will argue that protecting teachers’ free time without agendas is healthy and fosters more collaboration than any *synergy-session ever has.* There. I said it.

Regardless, no teacher should have their head in a barrel. Teaching has always been a craft passed sideways, from Pinterest to the colleague who heard you vent and said: “I had that same problem last year.”

Make every one of those moments formal, accountable, scheduled and call them Professional Development, and you kill the casual information exchange that made them work. The ideas stop flowing, the doors close, and every teacher ends up alone in a room, reinventing what the person next door solved three semesters ago.

We give kids unscheduled free time because we know how cognition suffers from extended focus. What about the teachers?
